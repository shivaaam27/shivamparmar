Sooner or later most business owners ask whether they need an app. Often it starts when a competitor appears in the App Store, or a customer asks, "Do you have an app?" It is a sensible question, and the answer changes from one business to another.

For most businesses the answer is a website first. An app earns its place when your customers, or your own staff, will use it often. Both are good tools. They do different jobs, and choosing the wrong one first can cost money you would rather spend once.

## The difference in plain words

A **website** opens in the browser on any phone or computer. People find it through a search, a link or a message, and there is nothing to install.

An **app** is installed from the App Store on an iPhone or from Google Play on an Android phone. It sits on the home screen, can send notifications, can use the camera and the location, and can keep working when the connection is poor.

## When a website is enough

- Customers find you by searching, and want to see what you do, where you are and how to reach you.
- You take enquiries, bookings or orders from time to time, not every day.
- People use your service rarely, for example a stay, a one-off purchase or a yearly service.

A well-built website can also behave like an app. It can be added to the home screen, open quickly and take payment by mobile money. Many needs that sound like an app are met this way, at a lower cost and with nothing for your customers to download.

## When an app is worth it

- People will use it every week or every day: ordering, memberships, loyalty, deliveries or learning.
- Your own team uses it for daily work in the field, such as drivers, inspectors or stock counts, especially where the connection is unreliable and the app must keep working without it.
- It needs the camera, a scanner, the exact location or notifications that customers must see.
- You want a permanent place on your customer's home screen.

## What an app costs beyond the build

- **Two platforms.** iPhone and Android each have their own rules, and the app must be tested on many different phones.
- **Store accounts.** Apple charges a yearly fee to publish apps, and Google a one-time fee. The accounts should be in your company's name.
- **Review.** Apple and Google check every release. They can refuse an app that is unclear about what it does or how it treats personal data, so time must be allowed for it.
- **Updates.** Phones change every year, and apps must be updated to keep working. Your customers also have to install each update, so older versions stay in use for a long time.
- **Support.** When something goes wrong, people will contact you. Someone has to answer them.
- **Running costs.** Most apps need a server behind them, and that is paid for as long as the app is in use.

## Security and privacy

- **Customer information.** Collect only what you need, keep it protected, and follow the data protection rules that apply in Tanzania. Both stores require a clear privacy policy.
- **Signing in.** Strong logins, sessions that close by themselves, and a way to sign someone out if a phone is lost.
- **Payments.** Handled by approved providers, for mobile money and cards. Card details are never stored in the app.
- **The keys.** The store accounts and the digital keys used to publish updates must belong to you. Otherwise you cannot update your own app if the developer is no longer available.
- **Backups and monitoring** for the server behind the app, and testing on older phones before every release.

## What good delivery looks like

- **One clear job to start.** Write down the single thing the first version must do well. A small app that is finished serves you better than a large one that is late.
- **A written scope and price,** with the stages and dates set out.
- **A design you can tap through** on your own phone before the building starts.
- **Stages you can see,** and testing on real phones.
- **Time for the store review** built into the plan, then a proper launch.
- **A handover** of the store accounts, the source files and the documents.
- **Support afterwards,** with a named person to call.

An app usually takes longer than a website, often a few months, so the dates matter.

## A simple way to decide

1. Will people use it more than once a month?
2. Does it need something only an app can do, such as the camera, working without a connection, or notifications?
3. Is your website already working well?

If the answer to the first two is no, a website is enough for now. If your website is not yet working well, fix that first. Many businesses start with a website and add an app later, once real use shows it is needed.

## Questions to ask before you agree

1. What will the first version do, and what is left for later?
2. Who will own the store accounts and the source files?
3. Will it be built for iPhone and Android, and how is each one tested?
4. How are customer data and payments protected?
5. How long does the store review take, and who handles it?
6. What will it cost each year to keep the app running and updated?
7. What happens if I want to move to another developer?
8. Who do I call when a customer reports a problem?
